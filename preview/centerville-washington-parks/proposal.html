Answers to vendor questionsQuestions 1 to 3

1. The three biggest opportunities for improvement

Athletic Field Status. It is the most visited page on the site, yet it is reached from a homepage tile and is not in the main menu. On October 3 it was a single list of 33 fields in one column with no grouping and no summary, about nine phone screens long. An automated accessibility check found 15 color-contrast failures on that page, more than on any other page we tested. Grouping by park, a count at the top and a sport filter would shorten the most common visit on the site to a glance.

The Park Finder. Staff already keep a clean list of 38 features for 51 parks. The finder shows only park names in return, so a resident opens page after page to compare. The same data can answer the question directly: which parks, where, how far, and what each one has.

Programs. Every program is entered in RecDesk and again on the website, about 60 staff hours a year by your estimate, and the two copies drift apart. One source would remove the retyping and the customer-service calls that follow a mismatch.

2. What we would retain

  • The brand. The logo, the letterhead colors, and "Explore your community's BIG backyard."
  • The way staff manage park features. Checking boxes on each park is the right model. We would keep it and make it drive more.
  • The structure of park pages. Hours, location, pet policy, about, features and history appear in the same order on all 51 pages. That consistency made the concept possible and should carry over.
  • RainoutLine for field status, exactly as staff use it today, and RecDesk for registration and payment.
  • DataYard hosting.
  • The monthly nature posts and the news section. A post about vultures was your third most visited page last year. That writing is an asset.
  • The alert banner, with clearer wording and a control staff can switch on and off.

3. How we would approach RecDesk integration

RecDesk stays the place where residents register and pay. The website becomes a reader of RecDesk, not a second copy.

  • Method. A scheduled job on the website reads programs through RecDesk's read-only API (the $600 per year connection the District confirmed) and stores them as "Program" entries in the CMS. Residents search those entries on cwpd.org and tap through to RecDesk to register.
  • What would display automatically. Title, dates and times, location, registration deadline, description, the registration link, and the photo if RecDesk supplies one. Each program is matched to its park by location name, so park pages list their own programs. In the concept, all 80 current listings matched to a park this way.
  • Full or canceled programs. If the API reports capacity and status, a full or canceled program leaves cwpd.org on the next sync. We would confirm that on the RecDesk test account during discovery, before design depends on it. RecDesk does not publish its API documentation, so we will not promise a field we have not seen.
  • If full automation is not possible. The fallback is a one-step import of RecDesk's program export, run by staff or on a schedule, which still ends retyping. Either way the customized calendar plugin is retired, so nothing on the site is frozen at an old version.
  • Limitations and upkeep. The connection is read-only. If RecDesk changes its API, the sync needs an update, which the maintenance plan covers. The sync is monitored and we are alerted if it fails. Facility reservations and the accessibility of the RecDesk portal are outside this work, as the addendum states.
  • Our experience. We have not integrated with RecDesk before. We have built this kind of scheduled read from one system into another, and we build recreation registration software ourselves, so the data is familiar.

5. How we will achieve WCAG 2.1 AA compliance

By building it into the templates, testing it by hand, documenting it, and fixing what your independent auditor finds at no added cost.

  • Design. Colors are chosen against measured contrast. The brand green and rust are kept for large type and decoration. Body text and buttons use the deep green, which passes on white.
  • Build. Real headings, landmarks, labels and focus states. Menus, filters and the map work with a keyboard. Every map has a list beside it with the same information. Status is never shown by color alone.
  • Automated testing. axe and WAVE on every template, at desktop and phone sizes.
  • Manual testing. Keyboard only. Screen readers (VoiceOver on Mac and iPhone, NVDA on Windows). 200% zoom and a 320-pixel screen. Reduced motion. Automated tools find only part of the problems, so this step is not optional.
  • Documentation. A conformance report listing each WCAG 2.1 A and AA success criterion, how it was tested and the result, delivered before launch.
  • Third-party audit. We remediate the auditor's findings in our templates and code. This is included in the fixed price.
  • Staying compliant. Training on alternative text, headings, link text and PDFs. The maintenance plan includes a scan each quarter. PDFs remain with District staff, as the addendum says. We do not have a PDF remediation product and will not claim one.

For reference, on October 3 we ran the same automated check on six pages of the current site. Every page had a menu button with no name for screen readers and a setting that blocks pinch-zoom. Pages weighed 2.5 to 4.7 MB on a phone. These are the kinds of issues a new theme removes on day one.

Answers to vendor questionsQuestions 6 to 9

6. How we will preserve SEO during migration

  • Keep addresses that work. Park pages stay at cwpd.org/parks/park-name/. Four of your ten most visited pages are park pages.
  • Redirect everything else. We inventory all 307 pages and roughly 300 posts, and every address that changes or is retired gets a permanent redirect to its closest replacement.
  • Carry over what search engines read. Titles, descriptions, headings and image text, improved where they are thin.
  • Structured data. Parks, events and the organization are marked up so search and answer engines can read hours, addresses and dates directly.
  • AI search. You noted ChatGPT is quoting old information. Much of that likely comes from old pages still published as current. Dating every news item clearly, retiring outdated pages and giving each park one authoritative page are what correct it.
  • Watch after launch. Google Search Console and Analytics are monitored for 90 days, and any missed address is fixed.

7. What we would change about the information architecture

You are right that the foundation is sound. We would change four things, and test each with residents before committing.

  • Put top tasks where people look. Field status, find a park, programs and shelter reservations become the first choices on every screen size.
  • Turn press releases into dated news. 153 of the 307 published pages are archived press releases stored as pages. As dated news posts they leave the page tree, become searchable by date, and stop competing with current information.
  • One "Support" section. Donate and Volunteer sit together, with the Foundation linked prominently and living at its own address.
  • Careers, forms and contact within reach on a phone. You named careers as hard to find on mobile. These utility links move into the mobile menu and the footer.

We would confirm the menu with a short sorting exercise and a find-it test with residents of different ages and abilities, recruited with your help.

Content migration approach

  • Inventory. Every page, post and file in a shared sheet with its address and its visits, so decisions are made on evidence.
  • Decide. Staff mark each item keep, combine or retire. We flag what looks outdated or duplicated.
  • Move. Park pages are imported into the new template field by field, then checked one by one. News and nature posts are moved with their dates and images.
  • Start fresh on events. No past events are migrated, as the addendum states. Programs come from RecDesk for the current season.
