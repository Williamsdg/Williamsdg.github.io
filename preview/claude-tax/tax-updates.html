What changed for tax year 2025

Many temporary provisions from the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act were made permanent, and some new deductions were introduced. Here are the key changes for returns filed in 2026.

Federal tax changes

New deductions

Available to both itemizing and non-itemizing taxpayers.

  • Tips and overtime. A deduction for qualified tips (up to $25,000) and qualified overtime compensation (up to $12,500), subject to income phase-outs.
  • Senior deduction. An additional $6,000 deduction for taxpayers aged 65 and older, also subject to income limits.
  • Vehicle loan interest. A deduction (up to $10,000) for interest paid on certain passenger vehicle loans taken out in 2025.

Credits and limits

  • SALT cap increase. The itemized deduction limit for state and local taxes is temporarily increased to $40,000 per household (up from $10,000) for 2025 through 2029, with phase-outs for high earners.
  • Child Tax Credit. The credit is increased to $2,200 for qualified taxpayers.
  • EV credit. The Electric Vehicle tax credit was scheduled to end on September 30, 2025.
Part II

Social Security & Medicare

Adjustments that affect retirees and beneficiaries.

  • Social Security COLA. The Cost-of-Living Adjustment for 2025 is 2.5%.
  • Full retirement age. For those born in 1959 it is now 66 years and 10 months.
  • Earnings limit increase. You can earn more from work while collecting Social Security benefits before benefits are reduced by the earnings test.
  • Medicare Part D out-of-pocket cap. A new annual cap limits how much beneficiaries have to spend on prescription drugs out of pocket, and the “donut hole” coverage gap is eliminated.
  • Expanded mental health coverage. Medicare has expanded its list of covered mental health professionals to include licensed professional counselors and marriage and family therapists.
Part III

Paper refund checks are being phased out

IRS news release IR-2025-94, Sept. 23, 2025

The IRS, working with the U.S. Department of the Treasury, announced that paper tax refund checks for individual taxpayers are being phased out beginning September 30, 2025, as required by Executive Order 14247, to the extent permitted by law. It is the first step of a broader transition to electronic payments.

Why the change

  • Protect taxpayers. Paper checks are over 16 times more likely to be lost, stolen, altered or delayed than electronic payments. Direct deposit also avoids a refund check being returned to the IRS as undeliverable.
  • Speed up refunds. Electronic refunds are issued in less than 21 days if you file electronically, choose direct deposit and there are no issues with the return. Refunds sent by mail may take 6 weeks or longer.
  • Cut costs. Electronic payments are more efficient and cost less than paper.

What this means for you

  • Filing stays the same. Continue to file your return as you normally would.
  • Refunds go digital. Most refunds will be delivered by direct deposit or other secure electronic methods.
  • Help if you don’t have a bank account. Options such as prepaid debit cards, digital wallets or limited exceptions will be available.
  • Act now. Make sure you know your banking information, or consider opening a free or low-cost account.

During the 2025 filing season the IRS issued more than 93.5 million refunds to individual filers, and 93% of them, almost 87 million, went out by direct deposit. Executive Order 14247 also applies to payments made to the IRS; continue to use existing payment options until further notice.

Have your bank routing and account numbers ready so your refund can be direct-deposited.
