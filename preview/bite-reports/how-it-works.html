What goes into it

The long version, in Brad's words.

Pressure and fronts+

Crappie respond to pressure changing more than to pressure being high or low. We track the trend across each day of the forecast — falling, bottoming out, rising, or flat — and line it up against when a front is expected to reach your region. That gives us the window ahead of the front, the dead period during passage, and the recovery afterward.

It also gives us the bluebird day: the morning after a front clears, when pressure spikes and the sky goes bright and empty. It looks like the nicest day of the week and it is frequently the worst one to fish. We call it by name every time it shows up.

Wind — direction, not just speed+

“Windy” is not a forecast. A 12 mph wind pushing into a bank stacks bait against it and can turn an average spot into the best one on the lake. The same wind off your back on the same day does nothing. We read speed and direction together, and flag the days when wind is strong enough to override an otherwise promising setup.

Moon — weighted, not worshipped+

We use phase and the major and minor periods, and we pay attention to when those periods land relative to sunrise and sunset. A major period overlapping first light with pressure already falling is about as good as it gets.

What we don't do is let the moon outvote everything else. When the solunar tables say go and the conditions say don't, we tell you not to go.

Water temperature and the spawn+

From late winter into spring, water temperature is a major factor. It decides whether fish are staging on the first drop, pushing into the backs of the creeks, or already on the beds — and that decides where you should be fishing and how shallow.

We track where each lake sits and, more usefully, how far ahead or behind its own normal schedule it is running this year. A warm February moves the spawn up by more than most people expect, and your lake's timing is not your neighboring lake's timing.

Current and generation+

On lakes where a dam controls the water, flow can matter more than weather. Heavy generation moves fish, changes the depth they hold at, and can undo a forecast built on pressure alone. Where that applies, we say so plainly instead of pretending the weather call is the whole picture.

Your lake, not a region average+

A deep highland reservoir and a shallow oxbow forty miles apart respond to the same cold front on different schedules — and neither reacts the way a statewide average suggests. Smaller and shallower water turns faster. Bigger and deeper water lags.

Fronts also don't hit an entire state at once. A system reaching north Alabama Tuesday afternoon may not clear the southern end until Wednesday night, which shifts every window with it. Reports are built lake by lake, with that regional lag accounted for.

Week two, and being honest about it+

Weather models get less reliable past about a week. We still give you the second week, because that is when you actually book time off — but we label the confidence and keep the calls directional rather than pretending we know the hour.

When the data isn't there, the report says the data isn't there. It does not fill the gap with something that reads like a forecast.

The result is a two-week outlook that tells you which mornings are worth the drive, which ones to skip, and why — for the lakes you actually fish.
