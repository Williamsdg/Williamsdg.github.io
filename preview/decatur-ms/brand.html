Town of Decatur · Newton County, Mississippi

Decatur has more identity than it knows.

A flag almost nobody remembers, a motto cast into a wall downtown, and a legal duty to hold a seal it does not appear to have. This is what exists, what the law requires, and ten ways forward.

Where things stand

What the Town has today

Three findings, and the third is the one to lead with.

A wordmark, and only that

“DECATUR” in a high-contrast Didone serif over letterspaced “MISSISSIPPI”. It is the only graphic mark anywhere on the Town’s website — and it is a white-only PNG, so there is no vector, no version for a light background, and nothing usable as a favicon or a patch.

A flag — yes, really

A gold, white and gold triband reading DECATUR over “Committed to Faith · Family · Education.” Recorded by Flags of the World and photographed at a Mississippi Municipal League conference. It is not on the Town’s website. The adoption date is unknown.

No seal at all

Neither the Town nor Newton County has one. The place a seal should sit — the medallion at the centre of the police vehicle badge — is a blank gold disc on a stock catalogue decal.

The clearest way to show this to the Board: enlarge a photograph of the Town’s own police door. The empty circle makes the whole case without a word being said.
One more open slot. Decatur has two water towers. Both are plain white and neither carries a single letter. That is the cheapest, highest-visibility place a new mark could ever go.

Not a vanity project

Mississippi law already requires a seal

This matters more than any design argument, particularly with a board that is watching cost.

“The clerk shall be the custodian of the municipal seal, and each municipality shall adopt and provide a seal.”

Miss. Code Ann. § 21-15-17

The seal is not decoration. It is what makes a Town document self-proving. Mississippi courts have held that an ordinance must be verified by the clerk’s certificate and the seal of the town to be admissible — Dennis v. Walnut Grove (1930) and In re Hazlehurst (1963). The seal is also legally operative on warrants and cheques (§ 21-39-13), bonds (§ 21-33-315), tax-redemption releases (§ 21-33-61) and documents the Mayor executes (§ 21-7-13).

No design is prescribed. Mississippi tells the Town it must have a seal and says nothing whatever about what it should look like. The Board has complete latitude.
A second obligation, same piece of work. § 25-1-87 requires municipal vehicles to carry compliant markings, and non-compliance puts the Town’s sales-tax and fuel-tax distributions at risk. A proper mark with a vehicle specification satisfies that too — alongside the April 2028 website accessibility deadline. Three duties, one project.

Before any drawing

There is one decision only the Town can make

Decatur currently has two different identities running at once, and they do not agree.

The flag says gold and black

Gold bands, black fimbriation, black Didone lettering. It carries the motto and it is genuinely the Town’s.

But gold and black is also East Central Community College, headquartered in Decatur. Leaning gold risks the Town reading as a college department.

The website says brick red

#a32a00, used twelve times in the site’s own stylesheet. It matches the brick of the welcome monument, the outline on the mural and the 1972 courthouse.

And it sits clearly apart from the county’s red, the high school’s orange and blue, and ECCC’s gold.

Our recommendation: build on the brick. It is already in use, it is defensibly Decatur’s, and it is the one colour in the area nobody else owns. If the Town would rather honour the flag’s gold, that is a perfectly good answer — it just needs to be a deliberate choice, made out loud, with the ECCC adjacency acknowledged.

The options

Ten directions

Each is shown large, then at 42 px, 20 px and reversed on dark — because that is where municipal marks usually fall apart. Every one is a single-colour vector, so it survives an embosser, an embroidery machine and a photocopier.

01

The Arch

Recommended

The one form that recurs independently across the welcome monument, the 1912 courthouse entry, the 1913 jail parapet and the 1930 gymnasium arcade.

Why it fits: Four separate Decatur sources, and it reduces cleanly to a one-inch embossing die — which a modern colonnade never will.

02

Courthouse Seal

Traditional

A conventional civic roundel: pediment, six columns, five stars for the five wards.

Why it fits: What most people picture when they hear "town seal." Six columns matches the real courthouse; the pediment is a stylisation.

03

The Crossroads

Strong

Four roads meeting at a junction, drawn in plan.

Why it fits: "Decatur Crossroads" is attested on the state Sherman marker. Literal, historic, abstract, and legible at an inch.

04

The Oak

Strong

The spreading oak from the welcome sign and the centre of the mural.

Why it fits: Drawn as an inland oak. A live oak would be a visible error — that species grows only in coastal Mississippi.

05

The Commodore

Distinctive

A four-point star for Stephen Decatur, the naval officer the town is named after.

Why it fits: Nobody local uses it, it scales further down than anything else here, and it is unmistakably about this name.

06

Brick D

Distinctive

A monogram built from brick courses.

Why it fits: Every courthouse Decatur lost was rebuilt in brick. "NC" belongs to the high school; "D" is free.

07

Civic Badge

Practical

A shield carrying the courthouse.

Why it fits: Made for the places a mark actually has to live: a police door, a decal, a water tower.

08

Five Wards

Modern

Five wedges, one per ward, meeting at the centre.

Why it fits: Abstract and contemporary. Says "this is one town made of five parts" without depicting anything.

09

Pine & Creek

Landscape

Loblolly pine above moving water.

Why it fits: 37,597 acres of farm woodland is the county’s largest land use, and there is a Decatur street called Loblolly Ridge.

Type

The thread already running through everything

A high-contrast Didone serif appears on the flag, on the current website wordmark, on the welcome monument and on the mural. It is the Town’s single consistent visual asset across more than fifteen years. Any new identity should keep it rather than replace it.

Rules

What any Decatur mark has to survive

Where it livesWhat that demands
An embossed sealPure vector, solid black, no gradients or tints. Every stroke heavy enough to hold at 1.5 inches; counters open enough not to fill in.
A cheque or certified copyLegible at three-quarters of an inch in one colour.
A police patchEmbroidery: nothing finer than about a millimetre, no lettering below a quarter inch. If the seal cannot do this, the patch gets its own lockup.
A vehicle doorA square version readable across a car park, in one colour against the paint.
A water towerOne silhouette, strong contrast, the name as display type — never as ring text.
A browser tabA text-free glyph that still reads as Decatur at 16 px.
The website, by April 2028SVG with the type converted to outlines, proper alt text, and any colour used for the name clearing 4.5:1 contrast.

Guardrails

What must not go in

  • No Choctaw-derived motifs without the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians’ written consent. Conehatta, in Newton County, is one of the Tribe’s reservation communities.
  • No likeness of Medgar or Charles Evers. Both were born in Decatur and the marker stands on the courthouse lawn. Honour it in narrative, naming and signage — not in a mark, and not without the family’s blessing.
  • Nothing from East Central Community College — the Warrior head, the “EC” monogram, the arch logo or the wordmark. All protected, and ECCC requires written approval for anything made off campus.
  • No school marks — the Cougar paw and “NC” monogram, or the Academy’s star-through-N.
  • No state flag or Great Seal device inside the mark, and no federal insignia.
  • No live oak and no Spanish moss. That species grows only in coastal Mississippi — Newton County is around 150 miles inland. Locals would spot it immediately.
  • No cotton. Newton County ranked 36th of 60 in 1860 and moved to corn after the war. Crops are three per cent of farm sales today; poultry is ninety-three.
  • Nothing Confederate. Decatur’s courthouse lawn carries a Medgar Evers marker and an all-veterans memorial, and no Confederate monument. That is an asset worth protecting.

Process

How a Mississippi town actually adopts one

1. Ask the clerk first

Whether a seal was ever adopted by ordinance, and when the flag was adopted. Decatur’s ordinances are not published anywhere online, and the clerk is the statutory custodian. Nothing should be designed over the top of something that already exists.

2. Show the Board the empty badge

Pair the photograph with § 21-15-17. This is a compliance item with a brand benefit, not a refresh for its own sake.

3. Gather a little public input

Even a short comment period. “We were never asked” is what sinks these projects elsewhere.

4. Adopt by ordinance, on the minutes

Artwork attached as an exhibit, the clerk named custodian, a short use policy, and a clause protecting documents already sealed.

5. Two marks, not one

A legally correct seal the clerk uses on cheques and certified copies, and a logo for everything the public sees. Keep them separate — conflating them is the usual mistake.

6. Phase the rollout

Signage and vehicles as they come up for replacement rather than all at once. Budget nothing for merchandise in year one.

For scale: small towns commonly pay between $6,000 and $15,000 for a seal, a logo and a brand guide, with the changeover phased over following years. Anything proposed here would sit well under that.
Two things we would verify before anything is adopted. That the Town’s courthouse is depicted the way the Board wants — the current building dates from 1972 and has no cupola, so a traditional seal is a stylisation rather than a portrait. And a clearance search, so the chosen mark is not close to something already registered.
