Town of Decatur, Mississippi · Newton County

She already built the fix. The platform won't let her finish it.

Mayor Rushing said she dislikes the layout and wants ordinances to be "just a hyperlink to a document." She was being modest. She has already written JavaScript to force it — and the vendor's platform makes the last step impossible.

Public site → Ordinances, both ways → Staff dashboard → Resident portal →

What she told us

Her words

"My biggest frustration is that I dislike the current layout. I have some experience with web design and the current layout doesn't meet our needs. If you look at our current site where all the ordinances are located, I dislike the format. I prefer just a hylink to a document/form." — Mayor Lea Ann Rushing, September 22, 2026

Three facts also came out of that email, and each one shapes the proposal:

  • They pay $720 a year for hosting, and the vendor created their .gov email addresses.
  • She has web design experience. She is not asking to be taught — she is asking to be unblocked.
  • "I don't think that's in our budget right now." She is pre-emptively declining the big version. The plan has to open with something small and real.

The finding

She wrote a script to fix the ordinance page herself

The ordinance page contains a hand-written block of JavaScript. It reaches into the vendor's component, pulls out every document, rebuilds each one as a plain underlined hyperlink, and then hides the vendor's original list:

const items = root.querySelectorAll("li.resourceItem");
…
link.textContent = name;
link.style.textDecoration = "underline";
…
// Hide the original Membershipware resource list.
if (grid) { grid.style.display = "none"; }

That is the entire complaint, expressed in code. It works today. It is also standing on the vendor's internal class names — li.resourceItem, .name, #resourcesGrid — inside a shadow DOM she does not control. One vendor update and the ordinance page silently goes blank.

And the half she couldn't fix is the worse half. Her links point at the vendor's document endpoint. That endpoint does not return a file. It returns a 115 KB HTML page that loads Adobe's PDF viewer from a third-party service to display the document. I tried every variant of the URL — there is no way to get a direct PDF out of this platform. The thing she asked for is architecturally unavailable to her.

Worse, every one of those 54 document pages is titled "Town of Decatur - Home." Every bookmark, every browser tab, every screen-reader announcement says the wrong thing.

Audit

What is actually working

Lead the call with this. She inherited more than she thinks, and none of it should be thrown away.

  • A real .gov domain with HTTPS — the hard, credibility-defining part is already done.
  • Sensible navigation. Government / Departments / Community / About is a genuinely good top-level structure.
  • 54 ordinances are digitised and online. Most towns this size have none. The corpus exists; only its presentation is broken.
  • The history page is the best writing on the site — 1836, Sherman, the 1912 proclamation, the town buying itself a college. It is buried three clicks deep.
  • The Mayor's welcome letter is warm and specific and names Turkey Creek, ECCC, and the schools.
  • Eight real forms are posted — building, zoning, road, privilege licence, water & sewer, leak adjustment, employment, comprehensive plan.
  • Online payments already exist for water and for court.
  • A business directory with 70 mapped businesses — a real asset nobody is pointed toward.
  • Staff and elected officials all have @townofdecaturms.gov addresses.

What is not working

  • Nothing is in the page source. Every list — ordinances, staff, officials, forms, news, calendar — is rendered by JavaScript into a shadow DOM. Google cannot index the ordinances. A resident searching "Decatur MS building permit" will not find the Town.
  • The News page is empty. "No news posts are available at this time." Every announcement lives on the Facebook page instead.
  • Financial transparency is one file — an FY25 budget from December 2024. No audits. No agendas. No minutes, ever.
  • The Explore page renders nothing for a visitor, despite 70 businesses sitting in the data behind it.
  • The public contact address is decaturpolicems@gmail.com — a Gmail address, and the Police Department's, used as the Town's general contact on a .gov site they pay for.
  • No <h1> on the homepage. Its only headings are hidden generic labels: "links", "content", "Posts", "embed".
  • Every page ships the same broken tag — </span< instead of </span> — and a meta description that is literally a single non-breaking space.
  • The "Court Payment" link is not a link. It is a scripted element with no real destination, so a keyboard-only user may be unable to reach the Town's highest-consequence action.
  • A typo in the Mayor's own letter — "ready to welome you" — that she cannot fix without the vendor.

Her question, answered

Two ways to read the ordinances — she picks

She asked for plain hyperlinks. Plain hyperlinks are correct, and they are the default. But a searchable library is also worth seeing before she decides, so the page ships with both and a switch between them. No argument, no mock-up debate — she clicks.

A

Simple list

All 54 ordinances grouped by subject, each one an underlined hyperlink straight to the document. Visited links change colour. Amended ordinances are flagged. This is the default view, and it is exactly what she asked for.

B

Browse & search

The same 54 documents with a search box and subject filters — Animals, Alcohol, Streets & Traffic, Water & Sewer, Zoning. Useful for the realtors, lawyers and insurers who are the real audience for an ordinance archive.

Both views share one improvement she cannot get today: the link is the document. One click, one file, its own page title, its own web address — and no Adobe viewer in between.

The theme is not decoration

Decatur already has a visual identity and it is painted on a wall downtown: a church steeple, a spreading oak, children reading beneath it, graduates throwing their caps into an amber sky, and the words Faith · Family · Education. The site's palette comes from that mural and from the Town's existing #a32a00 brick red. The 1912 proclamation in the Newton County Times is why the headings are set in a newspaper serif.

The demographic fact that should drive the design: Decatur's median age is 21.4 in a town of 1,945, because East Central Community College enrols more than 2,400 students here. The college is bigger than the town. But the students are not the utility customers — the site is built for the roughly 540 households who are.

Constraints that outrank aesthetics

Newton County is not a broadband county

Roughly one household in five cannot load the site at all, and one in four is on a phone with a data cap. That rules out heavy pages, PDF-first content, and anything that assumes a fast connection.

What was built against that: every page here is 7–14 KB compressed, with no images, no frameworks, no build step, and one external request for fonts. Every illustration is inline SVG. For comparison, a single ordinance click on the current site costs 115 KB before Adobe's viewer even starts loading.

It also means the site's job is partly to make the paper notice and the phone script cheap to produce. It does not replace them.

Raise these first

Three things that are urgent and have nothing to do with design

1 · The .gov registration expires November 3, 2026. That is about six weeks away. If it lapses, the website and every @townofdecaturms.gov mailbox stop working at the same moment. Ask on the call: is a Town employee a registered domain manager at get.gov with their own Login.gov account, and who receives the renewal notices?
2 · Who owns the mailboxes? The Town's mail runs on Google Workspace, and the vendor set it up. If the vendor holds the administrator account, they control creation, deletion, password resets and the mail itself — and Town email is a public record the Town is legally responsible for. The DNS zone also appears to sit in the vendor's Azure tenant. Nothing should move until mailboxes are exported and a Town-owned tenant exists. This is the real lock-in, not the website.
3 · $720 is a legacy rate. The vendor's current published pricing for a town in Decatur's population band is roughly $1,908 a year. "Do nothing" may not stay a $720 decision at renewal — which reframes the whole budget conversation in her favour.
Also worth knowing: the federal ADA Title II web rule requires WCAG 2.1 AA. For a public entity under 50,000 people the deadline was extended in April 2026 and is now April 26, 2028 — not 2027. If any earlier proposal said 2027, correct it proactively; it demonstrates you actually track this.

The plan

Phased so the first step costs almost nothing

She said the demo options are out of budget. So the plan does not start with the demo options.

Phase 0  Free, this month, builds trust

Things the Town can do without hiring anyone — several using modules they already pay for.

  • Confirm the .gov renewal and domain-manager seats. Deadline-driven.
  • Establish who owns the Google Workspace tenant, and export mailboxes.
  • Replace the Gmail contact address with a .gov address sitewide.
  • Adopt a written seven-working-day public records policy — without it the default response window is one working day.
  • Publish a twelve-month late-fee and cut-off calendar. Cheapest high-value page there is.
  • Start posting agendas and minutes using the vendor modules already included.

Phase 1  The build she can actually approve

The public site and the ordinance library — the thing she complained about, fixed properly, plus the pages that carry the Town's legal obligations.

  • New public site with the action grid, trash/limb/cut-off block, and real page titles.
  • Ordinance library in both views, every document a direct link with its own address.
  • Agendas, minutes, budget and audits published as accessible web pages, with the signed PDF alongside.
  • OCR and restructure the document archive — roughly 90% of the Town's posted documents currently have no text layer, and the budget is posted as an image.
  • Alerts by text and email, enrolled from the water billing file with opt-out rather than a sign-up form nobody fills in.

Phase 2  Staff dashboard

The part no website vendor sells, because it is Mississippi-specific: the statutory calendar, docket-to-agenda-to-minutes as one object, the records-request log with its clock, the service-request tracker, and the Municipal Compliance Questionnaire.

Phase 3  Resident portal, read-only over the billing system

Contingent on the billing vendor producing a nightly export. Account lookup, twelve months of usage, leak alerts, and a "did they already pay online?" screen so the Town stops disconnecting people who have paid.

What we should deliberately not build

  • Utility billing itself. It is the Enterprise Fund's receivables ledger with statutory receipt warrants, deposit liabilities and audit exposure. Purpose-built packages sell for under $1,500.
  • Card processing. Deep-link to the existing processors; taking cards pulls the Town into PCI scope.
  • Municipal court case management. Fines are a small share of revenue and the risk is disproportionate. Payment by citation number only — no public docket search.
  • A native mobile app. Birmingham's own 311 app has about a thousand installs for a city of 196,000. This town has 540 households.
  • Codification of the ordinances. Real work, $8,000–$11,000, and a legal product. Refer it out — but raise it, because the Town appears never to have adopted an official code.
  • Any claim that posting online satisfies legal publication. In Mississippi it does not. Ordinances, the budget and the audit synopsis take effect through the newspaper.

For the call

Questions only she can answer

  • Who at the Town is a registered .gov domain manager, and who gets the renewal notices?
  • Does the Town or the vendor hold the Google Workspace administrator account?
  • Are the ordinance PDFs scans or born-digital? This changes remediation cost by an order of magnitude.
  • Has the Board ever adopted an official code of ordinances, or is there only the chronological ordinance book?
  • Which company handles the water billing, and will they produce a nightly balance export?
  • Is there any unspent, obligated ARPA money? The expenditure deadline is December 31, 2026.
  • What does she personally want to be able to change without calling anyone?
Tone for the call. She is not a confused buyer — she diagnosed the problem correctly and wrote code to work around it. The winning line is not "here's what's wrong with your website." It is: "You were right, you already fixed the half you could reach, and the half you couldn't isn't your fault — the platform can't do it."
