The students who brought a 250-year-old cannon back to life

For nine months, eleven students at Riverchase Career Connection Center cleaned, machined, and reassembled a Revolutionary-era cannon barrel that now stands at American Village.

By Hoover Sun Staff Photographs by Erin Nelson May 19, 2026
Above: Senior welding student Caleb Houston inspects the cannon barrel before the final coat of preservation oil.

When American Village called Riverchase Career Connection Center asking if students could refurbish a 250-year-old artillery piece, no one was sure it was possible. Then they started.

The barrel arrived in October, wrapped in oily canvas and laid on a wooden pallet in the back of a flatbed truck. American Village had transported it from a private estate in Tennessee, where it had spent the last six decades resting in a tobacco shed. Riverchase Career Connection Center — the regional career-technical school known as RC3 — had agreed to take a look.

The barrel weighed 540 pounds. It was caked in surface rust, the trunnions had been crudely rewelded sometime in the 19th century, and one of the iron rings around the breech was cracked through. By every reasonable measure, it was a junkyard piece.

“The first time we saw it, two of our seniors basically held a funeral for it,” said welding instructor Brian Crum. “Then they came back the next morning and started taking measurements.”

The work

Over nine months, eleven students from RC3’s welding, machining, automotive restoration, and woodworking programs took on the project as a cross-disciplinary capstone. They documented every step in a 70-page conservation log that will be archived alongside the cannon at American Village.

The barrel was cleaned by hand, using soft brass brushes and period-correct preservatives — no media blasting, no power tools that would compromise the patina or the original tool marks. The cracked breech ring was removed, characterized in the materials lab, and replaced with a heated forge-welded ring that students fabricated themselves under the supervision of a master smith brought in from Tannehill.

These kids did something that two professional conservation firms told American Village couldn’t be done for less than $40,000. They did it as schoolwork — and they did it better. — Mark Davidson, Director of Programs, American Village

A new oak carriage was constructed by the woodworking program from white oak donated by a Shelby County mill. The carriage hardware — bands, pins, and iron stays — was hand-forged in RC3’s blacksmith shop. The final assembly happened over three days in early May, with the entire program watching.

The students

The students who led the work range from sophomores to seniors. Four of them have already accepted positions with skilled-trade employers in central Alabama; two are heading to Auburn for mechanical engineering. All of them have signed the inside of the carriage with a permanent marker — a tradition the project leaders started without asking anyone’s permission.
